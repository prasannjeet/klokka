Klokka, before we build it.

One clock, both sides: the employer logs the hours each employee worked, the employee sees the same month on their phone, and pay is a switch rather than a requirement. Product

What Klokka does, in plain words

A small employer with hourly workers opens Klokka every evening, taps each person's hours for the day, and is done. Every employee sees the same month on their phone the moment it changes. That is the whole product; everything else exists to make that loop trustworthy for both sides.

RoleWhoWhat they can do
EmployerRuns a cafe, cleaning firm, salon, shop or small agency with a handful of hourly workersCreates a workspace, invites employees, logs hours per employee per day, reads the insights, decides whether pay is shown, locks a month, exports CSV
EmployeeA worker at that business, on their own phoneSees their hours by day, week and month, the totals, earnings if the employer switched pay on, gets a notification on every change, can flag an entry, can share a monthly card
OperatorUs, running the hosted instanceSees workspaces, users, invite status, notification and email volume, service health. Never anyone's hours beyond aggregates

A workspace is one business. A person can belong to several (a worker with two employers, an employer who also works somewhere), with a different role in each.

The core loop

  1. Sign up as employer, create a workspace. Name, country and timezone, currency, week start day, a colour or emoji so several workspaces look different.
  2. Add an employee. Name, email, optional hourly rate. They get an invitation email, set a password, and land in the workspace as an employee. Until then they show as Invited.
  3. Log hours. Pick a person, a day, the hours: quick chips (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, full day), "same as yesterday", or the week grid that fills a whole team's week like a spreadsheet. One entry per person per day; every change keeps its history.
  4. The employee is told. Push and in-app notification when hours are added, changed or removed. A whole week entered in one sitting is one notification ("Nora added 5 days, 22.5 h"), not five.
  5. Both sides see the month. Calendar of hours per day, the month total, the average per working day, how it compares with last month. The employer sees it per employee and for the whole workspace.
  6. Insights. Employer: per-employee comparison, week-by-week trend, weekday distribution, busiest day, projected month end, days with nothing logged, labour cost if pay is on. Employee: hours this month, best week, streak, earnings if enabled.
  7. Pay is a switch. Off: Klokka is hours only. On: each employee has a rate and every hours figure gets a money figure next to it, on both clients.
  8. Close the month. Lock it (no edits without unlocking), export CSV per employee or for the workspace.

Small things that make it feel finished

  • Employee flags an entry ("I worked 4 h, not 2"); the employer fixes or dismisses; both are notified.
  • Notification center on both clients, with read state and per-user preferences.
  • Several workspaces per person, with a switcher.
  • Dark and light mode everywhere, following the device.
  • Avatars for people, a colour and emoji for each workspace.
  • Rounding rule (none, quarter hour, half hour) and a default day length for the "full day" chip.
  • A shareable monthly card ("your September: 142 h") as an image.
  • Opt-in weekly email digest, off by default.
  • Audit history on every entry, visible to both roles.
  • English and Swedish from day one, one shared catalogue for web, phone and notifications.

Deliberately not in v1

  • Employee self-logging or a clock-in timer
  • GPS, shift scheduling, invoicing
  • Payroll exports to Fortnox or Visma
  • Web push, SMS
  • iOS store build (no Apple account yet; the code stays iOS-ready)
  • Enterprise SSO
Parity is part of done.

Every feature ships on the API, the web app and the phone in the same ticket. A ticket that delivers one client is not done; deferring a client needs an explicit follow-up ticket. Both clients render the same numbers because totals and trends are computed once, by the API.

Design direction

Three directions were built; you chose Nightshift

Klokka means "the clock". The recommended direction borrows from the most famous clock design there is, the Swiss railway clock: one grotesk, black on white, and a red paddle for a second hand that sweeps and waits at twelve. Time is numerals, so a Swiss system makes the product's own content the design. The other two directions are complete too, and every mockup switches between them live.

SignalAa 22.5

White and near-black, one family (Archivo, expanded for display), International Orange as the only loud colour, hairline rules, huge numerals, no shadows. Trivially readable in both modes, cheap to render on any phone, and it keeps Kulram's motion without looking like Kulram.

Nightshift, chosenAa 22.5

Violet, magenta and cyan over deep night, Unbounded plus Inter, a glow on the primary, springy entrances. The visible descendant of the Kulram site. The fallback if you want the family resemblance at first glance.

ClayAa 22.5

Sand, terracotta and moss, Fraunces with Epilogue, big 28 px radii, a faint grain, gentle motion. Warm and friendly; the outlier if the other two feel too sharp.

Motion: four moves, used with discipline

  • Rise. Things enter by moving up a little and fading in, on Kulram's signature curve, revealed as you scroll.
  • Swap. A line of text flips to the next one (the "Made for the cafe / salon / cleaning crew" rotator).
  • Pop. Small things land with a tiny scale-up, staggered across a row: hour cells filling a week, chips, badges. Removal is a plain fade.
  • Paddle. The clock, synced to real time, the only always-on motion. Reduced-motion settings show the final state of everything.

What we keep from the Kulram site

The easing curve, the hero rise and stagger, the headline rotator mechanics, scroll-triggered reveals, count-up stats, the ticker marquee, the hairline card grids, alternating light and dark blocks ending in a giant cropped wordmark, chips as the interaction unit. What changes: the palette (Kulram is violet), the typeface (Kulram's slab serif says ledger; Klokka's wide grotesk says timetable), tight radii, no photography (the app itself, drawn in CSS, is the imagery), and a page built around the two roles instead of one promise.

Decisions the phone forced

  • Sign-in is one button. Logto opens in the system browser and hands back to the app, so there are no native password fields (same as Kulram's app).
  • Invitations are web-first. A new employee has no app yet, so the invite link opens the web join page, which then offers the app. Inside the app, an invite to a second workspace is accepted in place.
  • The week grid becomes one person per page, swiped left and right, with the next person peeking in; the spreadsheet stays on the web.
  • The share card never shows pay, only hours, whatever the workspace setting.
  • Android first, iOS-ready. No module without an iOS counterpart; the iOS build waits for an Apple account and runs in the cloud from this Linux host.
  • Distribution in v1 is a signed APK from the landing page; a Play Store listing is a later ticket. Package id com.prasannjeet.klokka, Firebase and Expo projects in place.

Backend

One API, one contract, two clients that cannot drift

Both clients talk to a single Java API. The API's contract is written first, as one OpenAPI file, and from it we generate both the server's interfaces and the one TypeScript client the phone and the web share. A feature therefore cannot exist on one client and not the other by accident: the types come from the same file.

Android appExpo SDK 57, React Native. Signs in with Logto directly, keeps the token in the secure store, calls the API with it.
Web appNext.js 16. Logto session in an encrypted cookie; the browser never holds a token, a server route adds it to API calls.
Klokka APIQuarkus on Java 25. Validates the Logto token, decides the role from its own membership table, computes every total, sends every notification.
PostgreSQL 18Workspaces, memberships, hour entries with history, flags, month locks, notifications, push tokens, preferences.
LogtoAccounts, passwords, the hosted sign-in page, organizations (workspaces), invitations, roles.
Expo push, FCM, SMTPPush to phones through Expo's service (Firebase on Android); invitation emails and opt-in digests through Migadu.

What was proven, not assumed

  • A throwaway Quarkus 3.39.5 project with every extension we need was created, packaged and tested on JDK 25 on this host: create 18 s, package 21 s, full test run green in 1 min 16 s (Postgres started automatically by the test tooling). Spring Boot is not needed.
  • The OpenAPI generator produced Quarkus server interfaces and a resource implementing them compiled on JDK 25. The same generation step publishes the contract to Nexus from CI (Maven and npm), so every client and the server build against one versioned artifact.
  • Quarkus 3.40 LTS ships this week; we start on it. Quarkus 4 (November) is a planned upgrade ticket, not a v1 concern.
  • Versions are pinned below "latest" where latest breaks things: ESLint 10 crashes Next's config today, TypeScript 7 is not supported by the lint tooling, and Expo pins React 19.2.3 for the whole repo.

Notifications engine

Every change writes a row to a notification table. A sweeper runs every minute: when a person's pending rows have been quiet for 10 minutes (or 30 minutes have passed since the first one), it folds them into one message ("Nora added 5 days, 22.5 h") and sends it as push plus in-app. A second job checks Expo's delivery receipts and drops dead device tokens. The weekly digest is a scheduled job keyed by a unique row per week, so it can never send twice. Text is rendered by the API from the same sv/en catalogue the clients use, in the person's stored language.

Data model

TableHoldsNotes
workspaceName, Logto organization id, currency, timezone, week start, show-pay flag, rounding rule, default day length, colour, emojiOne per business
membershipWho belongs to which workspace, as EMPLOYER or EMPLOYEE; display name, email, hourly rate, status (Invited, Active, Deactivated)The authorization source for every API call
hour_entryWorkspace, member, date, hours (two decimals), note, who created it, soft-delete markerOne row per person per day; edits replace, history keeps the old value

Every child table carries the workspace id with a composite foreign key, so workspace isolation is enforced by the database as well as by the code. Passwords are never stored: identity lives in Logto.

Accounts and roles, with Logto

How people get in, and what each one may do

Logto is the account system: it owns email addresses and passwords, renders the sign-in page (branded to the theme), sends the invitation email, and knows which organization (workspace) a person belongs to with which role. Klokka mirrors that membership into its own database and makes every permission decision from there, so a stale token can never grant access to a workspace someone was removed from.

EmployerCreates an accountLogto's hosted sign-up, email and password. Klokka gets a user id, never the password.
APICreates the workspaceMakes a Logto organization, assigns the EMPLOYER role, stores the workspace and the membership.
EmployerAdds an employeeName, email, optional rate. The API creates a Logto invitation valid for 7 days and the member shows as Invited.
LogtoSends the invitationThrough our mail account. The link opens app.example.com/join.
EmployeeJoinsSets a password, verifies the email, lands in the workspace as EMPLOYEE, is offered the Android app or the web.
APIMirrors and notifiesMembership becomes Active, the employer gets "Maria joined".

Roles

EMPLOYER and EMPLOYEE are per workspace (a person can be both, in different workspaces). platform-admin is a global role for the operator console and is checked on every operator route.

On the phone

Sign-in opens Logto in the system browser and returns to the app (the same flow Kulram's app uses today). The token lives in the secure store; the app never sees the password.

On the web

The session is an encrypted cookie. The browser never holds an API token; a server route adds it to each API call, which also means no cross-origin setup on the API.

Own Logto

Klokka runs its own Logto service on staging (klokka-logto.coolify.ooguy.com) in the Klokka Coolify project, with its own database and email connector. Nothing is shared with Kulram.

Hosting and releases

Each app is a Docker image in our Nexus registry, deployed as an image app in the "Klokka" project on the staging Coolify. What a push to main does

  1. Build and test only what changed: API (Maven, JDK 25), web app, landing, mobile, and the contract. Jobs run in parallel on the two runners.
  2. Publish the contract to Nexus: the generated server interfaces as a Maven artifact, the generated TypeScript client as an npm package, both at the API's version.
  3. Push images with an immutable tag and deploy staging by setting that tag on each Coolify app, so Coolify's own record names what is running; rollback is the same call with an older tag.
  4. Build and sign the Android APK (JDK 21, our keystore from a repository secret) and publish it to Nexus so the site's download link always points at the latest build.

Secrets (Nexus, Coolify, Expo, keystore, Firebase config) live in the GitHub repository's secrets and in gitignored local files, never in the code. Nexus and Firebase secrets are already set on the repository.

Developing on this host

  • API alone: Quarkus dev mode starts its own Postgres container; nothing to configure.
  • Whole stack: docker compose with Postgres 18 and Mailpit (catches every email locally); sign-in against the staging Logto with a localhost redirect registered.
  • Web: dev server on the LAN address (192.168.0.16) so a phone can open it; verified with a real browser, not localhost.
  • Phone: no emulator on this machine; Metro on port 8083, the debug APK served over the LAN and installed on your Android phone, exactly like the Kulram loop. Gradle runs on JDK 21 (JDK 25 breaks the native build step).
